Quantum K-Field

A public research archive exploring the geometric substrate beneath known physics

The Quantum K-Field is a research program developed by Krytronx and principal investigator P. Dwayne Esterline. It connects foundational physics, quantum gravity, emergent spacetime, celestial geometry, precision metrology, quantum sensing, optics, and advanced signal analysis within one geometric framework.

This repository is the public release of that work.

It contains the papers, experimental methods, instrumentation, datasets, software, geometric models, figures, and analysis workflows used to study the K-Field and its measurable relationship to physical law.

The central idea is simple to state:

The laws and constants of physics arise from a structured geometric substrate, and the motion and orientation of an observer determine how that structure appears in measurement.

Where the K-Field begins

The foundation of the K-Field begins with astronomical observation.

Galaxies have measurable axes. When those orientations are taken from the Siena Galaxy Atlas, expressed in the International Celestial Reference System, and projected onto a unit sphere, they form an organized directional structure.

Earth can be described in that same reference frame. Its position, rotation, orbital motion, epoch, laboratory orientation, and motion relative to the Cosmic Microwave Background can all be represented in ICRS coordinates.

This creates a direct geometric bridge between large-scale astronomical structure and terrestrial measurement.

From the galaxy-axis geometry, three associated plane systems are derived. Their intersections define an oriented root cell called Cell3. Cell3 preserves the directional and internal relationships recovered from the astronomical data.

The larger recursive and volumetric structure generated from Cell3 is called the Devon Dimension.

Together, Cell3 and the Devon Dimension form the geometric foundation of the Quantum K-Field.

From geometry to physical law

The importance of Cell3 is not limited to its shape.

Ratios among its faces and internal structures generate linked relationships associated with the proton–electron mass ratio and the fine-structure constant. Those same geometric quantities propagate through a connected derivation chain involving the Bohr radius, atomic velocity, Rydberg constant, Hartree energy, classical electron radius, vacuum impedance, and related physical quantities.

A separate scalar branch produces the gravitational structure, including the base gravitational scalar and its observer-dependent rendering.

These results are not treated as isolated numerical similarities. They form a dependency system in which shared geometric origins produce families of physical relationships.

In this framework:

geometry produces stable ratios;

stable ratios generate physical constants;

reciprocal geometry defines interaction pathways;

recursive repetition produces higher-order organization; and

observer position and direction determine how the fixed structure is rendered in measurement.

The K-Field therefore connects domains that are usually studied separately: gravitation, electromagnetism, atomic physics, astronomy, metrology, optics, materials science, timing, sensing, and signal analysis.

The Lucas Limit

The same geometry extends into a finite boundary graph called the Lucas Limit.

The Lucas Limit contains a q-spectrum, polarity laws, residual-shell structure, the Emily Eigen-spectrum, and the Jack Jacobian. Together with the scalar branch, it forms an over-determined derivation chain supported by more than 29 interlocking witnesses.

This branch extends the K-Field from root geometry into higher-order organization, finite boundaries, polarity, recursion, and spectral structure.

Experimental research

A major objective of the K-Field program is to connect fixed celestial geometry with measurable laboratory response.

The experimental work includes several complementary instrument classes.

Precision oscillator metrology

Orthogonally oriented oven-controlled crystal oscillators are compared through heterodyne and phase-sensitive measurement. These instruments are used to examine directional frequency differentials, celestial modulation, long-duration recurrence, phase reversal, and time-frequency structure.

Quantum spectrophotometry

Rotating multispectral instruments measure wavelength-resolved response as the sensor changes orientation. The resulting data are examined for spectral-channel coupling, antipodal symmetry, photon-energy mapping, Bragg relationships, harmonic structure, polarization, and unit-sphere coherence.

Celestial trajectory reconstruction

The laboratory observer state is reconstructed in ICRS using Earth rotation, orbital and lunar motion, laboratory geometry, epoch, and the Cosmic Microwave Background velocity vector. This allows instrument measurements to be compared directly with the orientation and traversal of the K-Field geometry.

Geometric computation

Cell3, its plane systems, reciprocal structures, observer trajectories, and higher-order extensions are reconstructed computationally for dimensional analysis, visualization, simulation, and experimental comparison.
